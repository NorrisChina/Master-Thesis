\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis establishes that \gls{id}-based synchronization is not merely a bandwidth-saving technique, but a latency-critical enabler for edge-based \glspl{dt}, particularly in bandwidth-constrained environments. By shifting the communication paradigm from "data reconstruction" to "state verification," we demonstrated that the \gls{id} protocol effectively decouples the synchronization latency from the state dimension, offering a decisive advantage over traditional transmission when the channel bandwidth drops below 1 Mbps.

Our empirical evaluation provides a definitive verdict on the engineering selection between algebraic and cryptographic encoding mechanisms for edge deployments. While \gls{rsid} offers unconditional information-theoretic security, our results reveal that it suffers from severe field saturation effects at short blocklengths ($n_{ver}=4$), rendering it impractical for low-latency control loops. In contrast, the cryptographic approach using truncated \gls{sha} maintains a stable encoding latency of approximately $2.2~\mu s$ independent of the payload size. Consequently, unless the threat model explicitly demands information-theoretic secrecy, truncated cryptographic hashing represents the superior engineering choice for \gls{urllc} applications, balancing computational speed with robust collision resistance.

Furthermore, the introduction of \gls{kid} marks a fundamental paradigm shift from "bitwise exactness" to "semantic compliance." By relaxing the strict equality constraint to a semantic safety set $\mathcal{A}_K$, \gls{kid} aligns the synchronization protocol with the control-theoretic stability margins of the physical asset. Our Pareto analysis demonstrates that introducing a moderate semantic tolerance (e.g., $K=10$ for a state range of 100) can reduce the fallback frequency by an order of magnitude while maintaining a bounded conditional miss probability. This confirms that \gls{kid} successfully trades "unnecessary data freshness" for "operational stability," validating the efficacy of Goal-Oriented Communication principles in networked control systems.

Finally, we extended the evaluation from a static (i.i.d.) state model to a dynamic drift model in which the robot evolves as a random walk and the \gls{dt} uses a \gls{zoh} predictor. This time-correlated setting reveals a qualitatively different failure mode: a single collision-induced miss can persist across multiple ticks, producing extended boundary breaches and larger peak drift before recovery. As a result, risk must be characterized not only by instantaneous probabilities, but also by duration (how long the system remains outside tolerance) and magnitude (how far the drift grows before correction).

In addition, we evaluated scalability to LiDAR-scale payloads up to 5~MiB at $n_{ver}=16$. The measured verifier generation time $t_{ver}$ increases with payload size for both encoding mechanisms, with \gls{sha} remaining faster than \gls{rsid} in the software prototype. Importantly, at realistic bandwidths the dominant term in end-to-end latency remains the transmission time of the full payload; therefore, \gls{id} retains its primary advantage by reducing the frequency of full-payload fallbacks rather than eliminating computation.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

While this work has characterized the latency and reliability of \gls{kid}, several avenues remain to extend its theoretical depth and practical applicability in next-generation networks.

\subsection{Energy-Aware Identification Policies}
Current evaluations utilize traffic volume and latency as proxies for system efficiency. However, for battery-powered \gls{iot} devices, energy consumption is the ultimate constraint. Future work should explicitly model the energy--delay product, balancing the computational energy cost of hashing against the transmission energy cost of radio interfaces. By integrating the First-Order Radio Model~\cite{heinzelman2000energy} with real-time power profiling of edge hardware, we aim to derive an energy-optimal policy that dynamically adapts the tolerance $K$ and verifier length $n_{ver}$ based on the device's remaining battery life—tightening safety margins when energy is abundant and relaxing them to maximize operational duration under critical battery levels.

\subsection{Integration with 6G Physical Layer Security}
The current implementation relies on synchronized \glspl{prng}, which introduces a dependency on upper-layer clock synchronization protocols like \gls{ptp}. To align with the architecture of 6G networks, future iterations will investigate the integration of \gls{plkg}. By exploiting the channel reciprocity in \gls{tdd} systems, the robot and the \gls{dt} can extract common randomness directly from the \gls{csi}. This approach would turn channel noise into a synchronization resource, eliminating the need for clock synchronization and enhancing robustness against timing attacks in harsh industrial environments.

\subsection{Scalability to Multi-Agent Systems}
This thesis focused on a single robot--\gls{dt} loop. However, industrial scenarios often involve swarms of robots sharing a physical workspace. A critical extension is the development of semantic multiple access schemes, where multiple robots superimpose their identification codes on a shared channel. Future work will explore algebraic constructions that allow the \gls{dt} to verify the aggregate safety of the swarm directly in the coded domain, checking for "Semantic Inter-Robot Collisions" without decoding individual states, thereby scaling the \gls{kid} paradigm to massive multi-agent systems.

\subsection{Predictive Digital Twins and Adaptive K-ID}
The dynamic drift evaluation in this thesis uses a deliberately simple predictor (\gls{zoh}) to isolate protocol-induced effects. A natural next step is to integrate stronger predictors such as Kalman filtering or learned kinematic models, and to study how prediction quality reshapes both the masking risk (through the breach rate) and the collision risk (through the verification rate). In addition, future work should explore adaptive policies that tune $(K,n_{ver})$ online based on observed breach statistics and safety budgets, tightening the tolerance under persistent drift and relaxing it during stable operation to reduce bandwidth.

\subsection{Decoder Compute, Scheduling, and Quantization Sensitivity}
Beyond bandwidth and latency, practical deployments must also account for DT-side compute and memory. Since the acceptance set size scales as $|\mathcal{A}_K|\approx 2\lfloor K/\Delta\rfloor+1$, a direct implementation precomputes $\mathcal{V}_K$ by hashing $\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}_K|)$ states per DT update, and stores up to $|\mathcal{V}_K|\le |\mathcal{A}_K|$ verifier codewords. Future work should therefore characterize end-to-end performance under explicit decoder compute constraints (e.g., bounded hash evaluations per tick), and study algorithmic accelerations (incremental updates, caching, GPU offload).

In addition, the dynamic setting naturally suggests a scheduling-centric view: rather than only reporting per-tick risk, one can characterize the distribution of inter-transmission times (survival/CDF metrics) to answer questions like ``with 99\% probability, how long does the channel remain idle?'' Finally, because $\Delta$ is tied to sensor precision and DT computational budget, a systematic sensitivity analysis over $\Delta$ is needed to guide robust configuration across heterogeneous sensing modalities.

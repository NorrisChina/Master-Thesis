\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter reviews the theoretical transition from classical data transmission to identification-based synchronization and analyzes the encoding mechanisms required for its implementation. We focus specifically on the mathematical necessity of randomization to overcome structural blindness in finite-field compression. Beyond exact verification, we further examine the generalization of this paradigm to \gls{kid}, illustrating how relaxing strict equality constraints to semantic safety sets allows the synchronization protocol to accommodate the natural variability inherent in physical control loops.

\section{From Data Reconstruction to Identification}
\label{sec:id_theory_context}

In time-critical control loops, the utility of a \gls{dt} depends strictly on its synchronization with the physical asset. Conventional protocols treat this as a data reconstruction problem, where the robot periodically transmits its full state vector $S_t$ (e.g., joint angles and velocities) to the \gls{dt}. While robust, this approach ignores a critical redundancy: the \gls{dt}, utilizing its internal kinematic model, already possesses a high-fidelity prediction $\hat{S}_t$ of the robot's state. The communication task, therefore, is not to inform the \gls{dt} "where the robot is," but rather to verify "whether the \gls{dt}'s prediction is correct."

This shift in objective allows for a fundamental change in coding strategy, moving from Shannon's transmission capacity to the Identification (\gls{id}) capacity defined by Ahlswede and Dueck~\cite{ahlswede1989}. In transmission, every possible state must be mapped to a disjoint decoding set, limiting the number of distinguishable states to an exponential growth $2^{nR}$ relative to the blocklength $n$. In contrast, identification allows decoding sets to overlap, provided the intersection between any two sets is statistically negligible. This relaxation enables the number of identifiable states to grow doubly exponentially ($2^{2^{nR}}$), implying that a verifier of extremely short length can distinguish a practically infinite number of robot states, provided the system tolerates a vanishingly small error probability.

\section{Encoding Mechanisms and Structural Bias}
\label{sec:encoding_mechanisms}

To realize this theoretical capacity in practical edge computing, the encoding process must map a high-dimensional state space $\mathcal{S}$ (e.g., 64-byte float vectors) into a low-dimensional verifier space $\mathcal{V}$ (e.g., 8-bit integers). We examine two primary implementations: algebraic encoding (\gls{rsid}) and cryptographic hashing (\gls{sha}), and analyze their inherent risks of systematic bias.

\textbf{Algebraic and Cryptographic Implementations.} 
The \gls{rsid} scheme treats the robot's state vector $S$ as coefficients of a polynomial $P_S(x)$ over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$. The encoding process evaluates this polynomial at a specific point $r$, yielding the verifier $v = P_S(r) \pmod q$. Geometrically, this represents the state as a curve, and the verifier as a single sample point on that curve. 
Alternatively, the \gls{sha} approach approximates this behavior using cryptographic diffusion. The state is serialized and processed through the \gls{sha} algorithm, with the output truncated to the target bit-length $n_{\text{ver}}$. While \gls{rsid} offers rigorous algebraic bounds on collision probabilities, truncated \gls{sha} benefits from hardware acceleration on modern processors, making it computationally favorable for high-frequency control loops.

\textbf{The Problem of Structural Blindness.} 
A critical limitation of deterministic compression—whether via polynomial evaluation or hashing—is the existence of "structural blindness" or systematic bias. Consider a simplified case where the encoder uses a modulo operation (e.g., $v = S \pmod M$). If the deviation between the physical robot and the \gls{dt}, denoted as error $e = S - \hat{S}$, happens to be a multiple of the modulus $M$, the generated verifiers will be identical ($S \equiv \hat{S} \pmod M$). 

Even if the robot's state $S$ changes continuously over time, this specific error pattern $e$ might persist (e.g., a constant sensor offset or a parallel drift). In a deterministic system, the verifiers would consistently match despite the desynchronization, leading to a persistent Type II (Miss) error. The system becomes "blind" to this specific class of errors, rendering the safety check ineffective regardless of the robot's movement.

\textbf{Randomization as a Countermeasure.} 
To eliminate structural blindness, identification theory mandates the use of \emph{Common Randomness}—a shared seed $r$ (or salt) that changes dynamically with each transmission. 
In \gls{rsid}, changing $r$ corresponds to sampling the polynomial $P_S(x)$ at a different X-coordinate. In \gls{sha}, appending a time-variant salt ($S || r$) alters the avalanche dynamics of the hash function.
By varying $r$, the system fundamentally changes the mapping logic at every time step. Although a collision may occur at a specific seed $r_1$ for a specific error $e$, the probability that the same error $e$ causes a collision at a new seed $r_2$ is independent and uniformly bounded. This converts potential "permanent system failures" into "transient probabilistic events," which can be rapidly detected in subsequent time slots.

\section{Theoretical Limits of Compression}
\label{sec:theoretical_limits}

While the introduction of randomization allows for drastic compression, the output length cannot be reduced arbitrarily. The fundamental lower bound for the verifier length $L$ is governed by the algebraic properties of the state representation.

In the polynomial model of \gls{rsid}, a state vector of length $k$ (interpreted as a polynomial of degree $k-1$) can intersect with another state's polynomial at most at $k-1$ points. To ensure reliable identification, the size of the evaluation field $Q = 2^L$ must be significantly larger than the number of potential intersection points. If $Q < k$, the "collision points" could saturate the field, making it impossible to find a seed $r$ that distinguishes the two states. 

Therefore, a necessary condition for reliability is $2^L > k$, or logarithmically:
\begin{equation}
    L > \log_2 k
\end{equation}
This inequality represents the physical manifestation of the double exponential limit. It dictates that the verifier length $L$ does not need to scale linearly with the data size $k$ (as in transmission), but must only scale logarithmically. As long as the verifier space is large enough to accommodate the "roots" of the difference polynomial, the randomized \gls{id} scheme maintains a bounded error probability, enabling secure and efficient synchronization for \glspl{dt}.

\section{Semantic Verification: Adapting to Control Flexibility}
\label{sec:kid_application}

While standard identification provides a rigorous method for exact state verification, its binary nature—accepting only a single, precise value—often contradicts the operational flexibility required in modern robotic control. In many practical scenarios, the \gls{dt} does not need to enforce a strict equality constraint ($x = \hat{x}$) but rather needs to guarantee that the robot operates within a permissible semantic range. This nuance necessitates the adoption of \gls{kid}, which generalizes the verification target from a unique point to a semantic safety set.

Consider a practical example of a mobile robot navigating a warehouse. The control policy might specify a safe velocity corridor, such as "maintain speed between 40 cm/s and 60 cm/s." If the robot moves at 45 cm/s while the \gls{dt} predicted 50 cm/s, a standard \gls{id} scheme would flag this as a collision (mismatch) because the hashed values of "45" and "50" are distinct. This triggers an unnecessary data transmission to correct a behavior that is, semantically speaking, perfectly valid. \gls{kid} resolves this inefficiency by defining the acceptance region $\mathcal{B}$ as the interval $[40, 60]$. As long as the robot's state falls within this set, the \gls{kid} verifier confirms validity without consuming bandwidth for minor deviations.

This logic extends beyond simple velocity constraints to more complex spatial and interactive scenarios. For instance, in \gls{hrc}, the exact coordinates of a robotic arm are less critical than ensuring the end-effector remains outside a defined "human safety bubble." Similarly, for high-precision assembly tasks involving compliant mechanisms, the robot may exhibit minor high-frequency vibrations (jitter) that are mechanically acceptable. Standard \gls{id} would treat these micro-vibrations as errors, whereas \gls{kid} can be tuned to ignore high-frequency noise while still detecting significant trajectory deviations.

By shifting the verification paradigm from data exactness to semantic compliance, \gls{kid} aligns the synchronization protocol with the actual control objectives. It allows the system to filter out benign variability—whether it be speed fluctuations, sensor noise, or compliant jitter—and focus limited communication resources on detecting genuine safety violations. This thesis specifically investigates the implementation of such semantic verification using randomized encoding on edge constraints.

\chapter{Results and Evaluation}
\label{ch:results}
\label{sec:results_and_evaluation}

This chapter presents the empirical evaluation of the proposed \gls{id}-based synchronization protocols. We first characterize the system's fundamental parameters, computational overhead and collision probability, to calibrate the theoretical models. We then perform a sensitivity analysis to identify the bandwidth and prediction regimes where \gls{id} offers a latency advantage over traditional transmission. Next, we conduct a comprehensive parameter sweep of the \gls{kid} protocol, quantifying the safety-performance trade-offs and identifying Pareto-optimal configurations for deployment. We conclude with a dynamic evaluation based on the random walk drift model to highlight time-correlated risk accumulation.

\section{Empirical Parameter Verification}
\label{sec:empirical_params}

To instantiate the latency models derived in Chapter~\ref{ch:design_methodology}, we first experimentally verify the computational cost ($t_{ver}$) and the theoretical reliability limits ($p_{miss}$) of the encoding mechanisms.

\subsection{Encoding Latency (\texorpdfstring{$t_{ver}$}{t\_ver})}
The encoding time $t_{ver}$ sets the irreducible latency floor for \gls{id}, even when bandwidth is effectively infinite. We measured $t_{ver}$ over 100,000 operations for both \gls{sha} and \gls{rsid}. The results, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:tver_results}, reveal a distinct structural divergence between the two approaches.

    \label{tab:tver_results}
    \begin{tabular}{lrr}
        \toprule
        & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{Verifier Length ($n_{ver}$)}} \\
        \cmidrule(lr){2-3}

        \midrule

\FloatBarrier

\gls{sha} demonstrates robust stability; its runtime is dominated by the input block size and is effectively invariant to the output truncation length ($n_{ver}$). In contrast, \gls{rsid} displays a counter-intuitive behavior where increasing the verifier length decreases latency (e.g., $14.55 \to 3.79\,\mu s$). This is an artifact of the polynomial construction: a larger $n_{ver}$ allows more bits to be packed into each field coefficient, reducing the total number of coefficients ($k$) required to represent the payload.

    For RS2ID, the verifier consists of a small number of symbols over a field of size $q = 2^{n_{ver}}$; the dominant miss event is a coincidental symbol agreement, yielding the simple scaling $p_{miss} \approx 1/q = 2^{-n_{ver}}$. Importantly, this bound is not a function of payload size under the concatenated construction, so it does not exhibit the short-blocklength field-saturation behavior of the single-polynomial RS-ID model.

The results illustrate the fundamental trade-off between computation and communication. In the transmission-bound regime ($B < 1$ Mbps), latency is dominated by the physical transmission time ($T_{trans}$), and \gls{id} outperforms the baseline by compressing the required transmission from $n_{data}$ to $n_{ver}$. In the computation-bound regime ($B > 10$ Mbps), transmission becomes negligible and the latency floor is set by the encoding time $t_{ver}$; since the traditional scheme has zero encoding overhead, it eventually becomes faster. For large payloads (Figure~\ref{fig:latency_bw_4001}), the break-even point shifts to higher bandwidths, indicating that \gls{id}-based synchronization becomes increasingly valuable as data complexity grows.

\label{sec:lidar_scale_tver}
The baseline measurements above use small telemetry payloads (96 bits and 4001 bits). For high-dimensional sensors such as \gls{lidar}, the payload size can grow by orders of magnitude. We therefore extend the $t_{ver}$ benchmark to payloads up to 5~MiB (Section~\ref{sec:lidar_scalability_params}).

\input{tables/generated/tver_lidar_scalability_ci_nver16}

Figure~\ref{fig:tver_scaling} (visualized from Table~\ref{tab:tver_lidar_scalability}) highlights a clear crossover in computational efficiency governed by payload size. For small payloads ($< 100$~B), \gls{rsid} exhibits a slight performance edge (e.g., $1.81\,\mu s$ vs. $2.06\,\mu s$ at 96~bits), consistent with a small fixed overhead for polynomial evaluation while \gls{sha} incurs a startup cost due to message padding and the fixed compression routine. For large payloads ($> 1$~KiB), \gls{sha} significantly outperforms \gls{rsid} (e.g., about $2\times$ faster at 5~MiB). While both approaches scale linearly in the number of processed bytes ($O(N)$), \gls{sha} relies primarily on bitwise operations that are heavily optimized on modern CPUs, whereas the current \gls{rsid} implementation is dominated by memory-heavy finite-field multiplication table lookups.

Overall, for high-throughput sensor payloads (\gls{lidar}/video), cryptographic hashing provides superior scalability in this prototype implementation. For distinct control signals and small telemetry payloads, both methods remain computationally negligible (on the order of a few microseconds).

\section[K-ID: Quantifying the Safety-Performance Trade-off]{\gls{kid}: Quantifying the Safety-Performance Trade-off}
\label{sec:kid_eval}

Having established the baseline performance, we now evaluate the \gls{kid} extension, which introduces semantic tolerance ($R$) to filter benign deviations. We sweep the physical tolerance radius $R \in \{2, 5, 10, 20, 30\}$ and the verifier length $n_{ver} \in \{4, 8, 12, 16\}$ bits to map the design space.

\subsection{Mechanism Analysis: The Tolerance-Verification Interaction}

Figure~\ref{fig:kid_heatmaps} maps the full design space to decouple the roles of physical and cryptographic parameters. The heatmaps confirm a fundamental interplay: the physical radius $R$ establishes the performance ceiling, as indicated by the speedup metrics, while the verifier length $n_{ver}$ determines the residual safety floor.

\FloatBarrier

A critical insight from the Uniform model (Figure~\ref{fig:kid_heatmap_uniform}) is the fundamental distinction between the two mechanisms that yield zero empirical risk. The top row of the heatmap ($R=30$) demonstrates safety through physical immunity. Since this tolerance encapsulates the theoretical noise boundary ($a \approx 27.71$), the system relies on passive physical margins. Verification events are suppressed simply because the robot rarely violates the tolerance, resulting in massive speedups exceeding $127\times$ and zero risk due to protocol silence.

Conversely, the rightmost column ($n_{ver}=16$ for radii $R < 20$) achieves safety through probabilistic immunity. In these configurations, the robot frequently exceeds the tighter tolerances, triggering continuous verification checks as indicated by the significantly lower speedups. However, despite the high frequency of checks, the 16-bit hash provides sufficient entropy to suppress collision probabilities below the simulation detection threshold, yielding an empirical risk of zero.

Between these two extremes lies a transitional ``danger zone'' around $R=20$ when paired with weak verifiers. In this region, the tolerance is large enough to dilute the acceptance set $\mathcal{A}_K$, thereby increasing the collision probability per check, but too small to contain the noise boundary. Consequently, the system suffers from the worst of both worlds: frequent verification triggers and a high likelihood of collision ($> 10^{-3}$), unless compensated by stronger verifiers ($n_{ver} \ge 12$).

The Gaussian model (Figure~\ref{fig:kid_heatmap_gauss}) presents a stricter challenge due to the distribution's unbounded tails. Even at the maximum tolerance $R=30$, the ``Safe Harbor'' is not absolute; residual risks in the range of $10^{-4}$ persist. This confirms that for unbounded noise processes, reliance on physical margins alone is insufficient. Instead, safety must be actively enforced by increasing the verifier length. The data demonstrates that increasing $n_{ver}$ from 4 to 16 bits effectively suppresses the overall risk by orders of magnitude across all tolerance levels, with only a minor penalty in speedup due to the increased packet overhead.

The Pareto frontiers in Figure~\ref{fig:kid_pareto_final} reveal that the optimal design strategy depends fundamentally on the noise containment regime. 

For the SHA-256 implementation (Right Panels), the "Active Verification" regime ($R < 28$) forms a continuous curve in the lower-left region. Notably, the high-safety zone (Risk $< 10^{-3}$) is not populated solely by tight tolerances; rather, it includes configurations across the full range of active radii ($R \in \{2, 5, 10, 20\}$), provided they are paired with strong verifiers ($n_{ver} \ge 12$). In fact, the $R=20$ configurations (red markers) frequently dominate the tighter tolerances in this region because their wider physical margins reduce transmission frequency while the strong hash maintains safety. The curve extends rightward as the verifier strength decreases, illustrating a smooth exchange of safety for marginal speedup gains.

A distinct "Passive Safety" regime appears at the far right, formed exclusively by the $R=30$ configuration (purple marker). Because this tolerance exceeds the theoretical noise boundary, the protocol achieves a discontinuous jump in performance, with speedups exceeding $200\times$. However, unlike the active regime, this operating point incurs a fixed empirical risk floor determined by the noise distribution tails, which cannot be mitigated by increasing the verifier length.

Comparing the two algorithms, the SHA-256 frontier exhibits a significantly denser distribution of optimal points compared to RSID. In the active verification region, SHA-256 allows engineers to finely tune the verifier length to traverse the risk-speedup curve. In contrast, the RSID frontier (Left Panels) is sparse, often forcing a binary choice between ultra-conservative settings and the high-risk passive regime, thereby validating the architectural choice of truncated hashing for granular control.

\section{Dynamic Evaluation Results: Random Walk with Drift}
\label{sec:dynamic_results}

Building on the experimental design detailed in Section~\ref{sec:implementation_design}, this section presents the evaluation of the K-ID protocol under dynamic conditions. We first analyze the qualitative behavior of the protocol through a representative stress-test trace, followed by a quantitative statistical analysis of safety risks and bandwidth efficiency across the parameter space.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis: The Anatomy of Failure}
\label{subsec:trace_analysis}

To visualize the system's behavior under the stress-test configuration ($R=10, n_{ver}=12, \sigma=1.0$), Figure~\ref{fig:rw_timeseries} plots a 1,200-tick snapshot of the state evolution. The blue curve represents the robot's true state $S_t$ undergoing a random walk, while the dashed orange line indicates the Digital Twin's estimate $\hat{S}_t$. The green shaded band denotes the semantic safety set $\mathcal{A}_K$.

The trace explicitly confirms the protocol's bandwidth suppression mechanism. For extended periods (e.g., $t \in [200, 500]$), the robot drifts within the safety envelope without triggering transmissions, effectively masking minor fluctuations. The green markers indicate nominal operation, where a boundary breach triggers an immediate fallback and correction. However, the red crosses highlight the critical failure mode induced by the 12-bit verifier: simultaneous boundary breaches and hash collisions. In these instances, the protocol fails to detect the excursion, allowing the robot to "escape" the safety set. This visualization validates the theoretical concern that probabilistic verification, when challenged by continuous drift, can lead to transient periods of unobserved instability.

\subsection{Quantitative Risk: Persistence and Magnitude}
\label{subsec:risk_analysis}

Beyond the single trace, we quantify the statistical risk profile using the parameter sweep data ($K \in \{2, 5, 10, 20\}$). Figure~\ref{fig:rw_metrics} reports the 95th-percentile summaries for Breach Duration ($L_K$) and Maximum Drift.

Figure~\ref{fig:rw_metrics}(a) reveals the non-linear nature of dynamic risk. For weak verifiers ($n_{ver}=8$, blue line), the breach duration exhibits a sharp jump as the tolerance $K$ increases. This indicates that failures are not independent events; a hash collision at step $t$ increases the likelihood of a collision at step $t+1$ due to the high correlation of the drifting state. Consequently, a single miss often cascades into a persistent blind spot. In contrast, the $n_{ver} \ge 12$ configurations (orange and green lines) effectively suppress these consecutive failures, maintaining the breach duration near 1.0. This validates that while $n_{ver}=12$ carries a marginal risk of single-step misses (as seen in the trace), it successfully prevents the catastrophic long-duration failures observed with 8-bit verifiers.

Figure~\ref{fig:rw_metrics}(b) illustrates the physical magnitude of these failures. We observe a linear relationship between the tolerance $K$ and the maximum peak drift. This confirms that while increasing $K$ improves bandwidth efficiency (masking), it raises the stakes: when a failure eventually occurs (whether due to a miss or transmission latency), the accumulated error is proportional to the masking radius. Thus, the choice of $K$ represents a direct trade-off between communication frequency and the worst-case physical deviation.

\subsection{Efficiency Analysis: Inter-Fallback Times}
\label{subsec:efficiency_analysis}

Finally, we evaluate the bandwidth implications of the protocol through the distribution of \glspl{ift}. Figure~\ref{fig:rw_ift_cdf} plots the empirical \gls{cdf} of the silent intervals.

The results demonstrate a significant "right-shift" effect as the tolerance $K$ increases. For the tightest tolerance ($K=2$, blue line), the system transmits frequently, with approximately 99\% of intervals being shorter than 10 ticks, indicating near-continuous communication. In contrast, for a relaxed tolerance ($K=20$, red line), the curve flattens significantly, showing a median silence duration of approximately 50 ticks, with a "heavy tail" extending beyond 200 ticks. This statistical characteristic effectively transforms the continuous sensor stream into sporadic, bursty traffic. For network schedulers, the red curve implies that if $K=20$ is configured, the channel remains idle for substantial periods, allowing the vast majority of time slots to be reallocated to other devices without contention.

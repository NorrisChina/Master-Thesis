\chapter{Results and Evaluation}
\label{ch:results}
\label{sec:results_and_evaluation}

This chapter presents the empirical evaluation of the proposed \gls{id}-based synchronization protocols. It first characterizes the system's fundamental parameters to define the bandwidth and state synchronization boundaries where \gls{id} yields a latency advantage over traditional transmission. Next, it reports a parameter sweep of the \gls{kid} protocol, quantifying the safety-performance trade-offs and identifying Pareto-optimal configurations for deployment. Finally, it evaluates a time-correlated random-walk drift model to highlight risk accumulation in continuous operation.

\section{Empirical Parameter Verification}
\label{sec:empirical_params}

To instantiate the latency models derived in Chapter~\ref{ch:design_methodology}, an experimental characterization of the computational cost ($t_{ver}$) and the theoretical reliability limits of the encoding mechanisms was performed.

\subsection{Encoding Latency Analysis}

The computational overhead was evaluated through a parameter sweep over verifier length $n_{ver} \in \{4,8,12,16\}$ and payload size $n_{data} \in \{96, 4001\}$ bits in Figure~\ref{fig:tver_sweep_summary}. Each data point represents the average encoding time over 1000 trials, measured on a MacBook Air (2020) host system, equipped with an Apple M1 chip (8-core CPU) and 8 GB of unified memory. This platform operates on the ARMv8 architecture. All measurements were recorded under macOS 26.2 using both the optimized and reference backends for \gls{sha}-256, and the reference implementation for \gls{rsid}.

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{figures/plots/tver_sweep_summary.png}
    \caption{Comparative overhead analysis of \gls{id} mechanisms. For \gls{sha}-256, results are reported for both the optimized hashlib~\cite{9789759} and the reference high-level backend.}
    \label{fig:tver_sweep_summary}
\end{figure}

\FloatBarrier

The measurements show that \gls{sha}-256 maintains a nearly constant latency profile across all verifier lengths for a fixed payload size. This behavior is consistent with the Merkle-Damgård construction of the \gls{sha}-256 algorithm, which processes data in fixed 512-bit blocks. As the number of iterations depends solely on the input data volume and padding rather than the final truncation length, the computational burden remains invariant to $n_{ver}$. 

In contrast, \gls{rsid} shows improved performance as the verifier length increases. This trend is substantiated by a detailed processing analysis presented in Table~\ref{tab:encoding_latency_breakdown}. The computational cost of \gls{rsid} is governed by the total number of finite field symbols $k$ required to represent the payload. According to the \glsentryshort{rsid} construction principles, each symbol's capacity scales with $n_{ver}$, meaning that for a fixed payload size, an increase in verifier length leads to a substantial reduction in the total symbol count~\cite{9789759}. 

As evidenced by the measurements for a 4001-bit payload, the symbol count $k$ decreases from 501 to 126 as $n_{ver}$ scales from 4 to 16 bits. This reduction in the total number of operations effectively offsets the marginal increase in the computational complexity per symbol associated with larger Galois Fields. Consequently, for algebraic identification, higher precision configurations not only enhance reliability but also yield a net gain in execution efficiency.

A performance gap is observed between different implementation backends of \gls{sha}-256. The optimized implementation, which leverages the OpenSSL engine and direct byte-stream processing, achieves lower latency compared to the reference backend. In high-level languages such as Python, the reference implementation is subject to substantial serialization overhead when converting integer lists into the internal C structures required for hashing. This data handling cost dominates the total latency for larger payloads, suggesting that the efficiency of data passing between software layers can be as important as the algorithmic complexity itself.

\subsection{Theoretical Reliability Characterization}
\label{sec:reliability_limits}

Following the theoretical framework established in Section~\ref{sec:encoding_mechanisms}, we assume a unified reliability model for both encoding mechanisms. As derived from the distance properties of \gls{rsid} and the statistical behavior of \gls{sha}-256, the miss probability is structurally governed by the verifier length $n_{ver}$. Consequently, we apply the scaling law defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:pmiss_law} to characterize the expected collision rates:
\begin{equation}
    p_{miss} \approx \frac{K}{2^{n_{ver}}}, \quad K=1
\end{equation}

This theoretical bound yields a collision probability of approximately $6.25 \times 10^{-2}$ for $n_{ver}=4$ and $1.53 \times 10^{-5}$ for $n_{ver}=16$.

The intersection points between the \gls{id}-based curves and the traditional transmission baseline delineate the operational viability of the protocol. This performance boundary is primarily governed by the state desynchronization probability $p_{desync}$, which determines the frequency of fallback events. When the system operates in a high-consistency regime (low $p_{desync}$), \gls{id}-based synchronization yields significant latency reductions by substituting large payload transmissions with compact verifiers. However, as $p_{desync}$ approaches unity, the cumulative cost of frequent fallback transmissions and the fixed encoding overhead $t_{ver}$ renders the traditional scheme more efficient.

The relative advantage of the encoding mechanisms is further influenced by their respective computational profiles. Across the evaluated configurations, the higher encoding cost associated with \gls{rsid} results in consistently elevated latency curves compared to \gls{sha}-256 for identical $(n_{ver}, n_{data})$ pairs. This computational penalty interacts dynamically with the payload size; for small telemetry payloads (96 bits), the increased bit-budget for larger verifiers can outweigh the algebraic gains. Conversely, for larger sensor payloads (4001 bits), the reduction in \gls{rsid} symbol count becomes the dominant factor, allowing the protocol to benefit from larger $n_{ver}$ despite the increased tag length.

Overall, the results suggest that the latency advantage of \gls{id}-based synchronization depends on payload complexity: at low payloads it is more sensitive to encoding overhead, whereas at high payloads it is dominated by transmission savings and becomes less sensitive to moderate increases in $p_{desync}$.

This analysis, visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:latency_vs_ndata_backends_nver16}, assumes a fixed verifier length $n_{ver}=16$, a bandwidth of 5 Mbps, and a state desynchronization probability $p_{desync}=0.1$.

The experimental results show that for all tested mechanisms, the expected latency $E[T]$ scales linearly with the payload size $n_{data}$ on a log-log scale. This linear relationship indicates that the transmission time, which is proportional to $n_{data}$ during fallback events, remains the dominant component of the total latency as the data volume increases. Specifically, at $n_{ver}=16$, the performance curves of \gls{rsid} and the reference high-level backend of \gls{sha}-256 are nearly identical. Both mechanisms exhibit an expected latency of approximately $8.5 \times 10^{-5}$ s for a 4001-bit payload.

A constant performance offset is observed for the optimized hashlib implementation of \gls{sha}-256. Across the entire evaluated spectrum, the optimized backend consistently maintains a lower latency profile compared to the other configurations. 

Overall, the results indicate that for larger payloads the end-to-end latency is mainly limited by fallback transmission, and differences between encoding mechanisms become secondary unless an optimized cryptographic backend is available.

\FloatBarrier

The heatmaps in Figure~\ref{fig:kid_heatmaps} show that the tolerance radius $R$ sets the attainable speedup, while the verifier length $n_{ver}$ determines the residual safety floor. Analyzing the results vertically, an increase in $R$ leads to a consistent improvement in speedup across all configurations. This trend is driven by semantic filtering: a larger tolerance reduces the frequency of active verification events. The overall risk also tends to decrease (and can vanish within simulation limits) as $R$ increases. While a larger $R$ increases the conditional collision probability for a single check by expanding the acceptance set $\mathcal{A}_R$ (and thus its size $K=|\mathcal{A}_R|$), this effect can be offset by the rapid decline in the trigger probability $P(\text{trigger})$. In the Uniform model, the overall risk drops to zero at $R=30$ because the threshold encapsulates the noise boundary ($a \approx 27.71$), making the protocol nearly silent and reducing opportunities for collisions. In the Gaussian model, the long-tailed distribution ensures that $P(\text{trigger}) > 0$ even at $R=30$, leaving a residual risk of approximately $10^{-5}$ that must be mitigated by $n_{ver}$.

Looking horizontally, increasing $n_{ver}$ from 4 to 16 bits results in a slight decrease in speedup due to the added bit-overhead per verification check. Simultaneously, the overall risk exhibits an exponential decay. For a tight tolerance of $R=2$, increasing the verifier entropy suppresses the risk in the Gaussian model from 0.8 down to a statistical zero. This indicates that increasing verifier entropy is an effective way to lower the safety floor, particularly when tight tolerances require frequent active monitoring.

Taken together, \gls{kid} supports two complementary operating regimes. In the high-$R$ regime, the protocol can become largely silent when deviations remain within the tolerated region; the resulting efficiency depends on the noise distribution. In the high-$n_{ver}$ regime, safety is maintained through probabilistic filtering, which remains effective even when verification is frequent. These findings suggest that systems in predictable environments can prioritize larger $R$ to reduce bandwidth, while systems under uncertain or heavy-tailed noise require sufficient $n_{ver}$ to maintain reliable state synchronization.

Figure~\ref{fig:rw_metrics}(a) illustrates a downward trend in the undetected interval $L_R$ as the tolerance radius $R$ increases. This behavior is tied to the stability of the drifting state relative to the threshold. At tight tolerances (e.g., $R=2.5$), the system is sensitive to minor fluctuations, and the high correlation between consecutive steps in a random walk means that once the state crosses the boundary, it is likely to remain in a "breached" state for a sustained period. As $R$ increases, the criterion for a breach becomes more stringent. While a larger $R$ increases the induced acceptance-set size $K=|\mathcal{A}_R|$ (and thus the per-check miss probability $p_{miss}\approx K/2^{n_{ver}}$), extreme deviations are also statistically more likely to fluctuate back within the tolerance due to the stochastic nature of the drift, thereby terminating the recorded breach duration earlier.

The curves for $n_{ver} \in \{8, 12, 16\}$ appear close because, in this regime, the per-event miss probabilities are sufficiently low that the duration is primarily governed by the underlying random walk dynamics rather than by collision-induced cascades. The exclusion of $n_{ver}=4$ is necessary as its miss probability approximates unity for moderate $R$, leading to a saturation that obscures the comparative analysis of more practical configurations.

Figure~\ref{fig:rw_metrics}(b) illustrates the physical magnitude of these failures. Despite the decrease in duration shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rw_metrics}(a), the peak physical error scales consistently with $R$. This reflects the masking effect: a larger tolerance allows a greater magnitude of drift to accumulate before any verification is triggered. Furthermore, the $n_{ver}=8$ configuration exhibits a higher peak error compared to $n_{ver} \ge 12$, indicating that weaker verifiers are more susceptible to consecutive misses once the system enters the $|E_t| > R$ regime. This extends the time window for the drift to grow unchecked. The convergence of the $n_{ver}=12$ and $n_{ver}=16$ curves suggests that for this noise level, a 12-bit verifier already reaches a point of diminishing returns where the peak error is dominated by the masking radius $R$ rather than the probability of hash collisions.

Overall, the results show a trade-off between the duration and the magnitude of synchronization failures. Increasing $R$ reduces the average time spent in an undetected state, but it leads to larger physical errors when failures occur.


\subsection{Throughput Analysis: Full-State Update Intervals}
\label{subsec:full_update_analysis}

This section evaluates the protocol's effective throughput by analyzing the \gls{sri}, defined as the number of time steps between two consecutive successful state synchronizations. As illustrated in the data flow (Figure~3.3), a state restoration is not merely triggered by the physical drift exceeding the threshold ($|E_t| > R$), but is also contingent upon the verifier successfully identifying the desynchronization to trigger the $n_{data}$ fallback. Consequently, the \gls{sri} distribution directly quantifies the actual re-synchronization rate under combined physical and cryptographic constraints.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering

Figure~\ref{fig:rw_sri_cdf} reports the empirical \gls{cdf} of the \gls{sri} across varying tolerances. For the $n_{ver}=8$ configuration, the $R=20$ case is excluded from the analysis due to entropy saturation: at a state resolution of $\Delta=0.1$, the induced acceptance-set size is $K=|\mathcal{A}_{R=20}|=2\lfloor 20/0.1 \rfloor + 1 = 401$, which exceeds the total verifier space ($2^8=256$) provided by an 8-bit verifier. In this regime, the theoretical miss probability $p_{miss}$ exceeds unity, rendering the verification mechanism mathematically incapable of reliable detection and causing the synchronization process to break down.

A comparison between Figure~\ref{fig:rw_sri_cdf}(a) and (b) reveals distinct trends in synchronization behavior. Within each sub-figure, an increase in $R$ results in a pronounced right-shift of the \gls{cdf} curve. This quantifies the traffic sparsification effect of the masking mechanism, where a larger physical tolerance reduces the frequency of boundary crossings and, by extension, the baseline rate of full-state updates. However, comparing the two verifier lengths shows that the 8-bit verifier exhibits a significantly shallower slope and a heavier tail. This indicates that even when the drift exceeds $R$, there is a high probability of hash collisions (misses) that delay the fallback, forcing the system to wait for subsequent verification events to resolve the error.

In contrast, the 16-bit verifier curves converge rapidly toward 1.0 shortly after crossing their respective $R$ thresholds. With $p_{miss}$ reduced to negligible levels, the \gls{sri} distribution becomes almost entirely governed by the stochastic timing of the physical drift rather than by collision-induced delays. This indicates the role of verifier entropy in reducing collision-induced uncertainty in recovery timing.

Overall, the \gls{sri} distribution illustrates a two-tier control logic in the \gls{kid} protocol: the physical tolerance radius $R$ regulates the base synchronization rhythm, while the verifier length $n_{ver}$ determines the reliability of recovery once $|E_t| > R$. Since the acceptance-set size $K=|\mathcal{A}_R|$ grows with $R$ and quantization $\Delta$, $n_{ver}$ should be scaled accordingly to avoid long-tail synchronization gaps caused by verification failures.

\chapter{Results and Evaluation}
\label{ch:results}
\label{sec:results_and_evaluation}

This chapter presents the empirical evaluation of the proposed \gls{id}-based synchronization protocols. We first characterize the system's fundamental parameters—computational overhead and collision probability—to calibrate the theoretical models. We then perform a sensitivity analysis to identify the bandwidth and prediction regimes where \gls{id} offers a latency advantage over traditional transmission. Next, we conduct a comprehensive parameter sweep of the \gls{kid} protocol, quantifying the safety-performance trade-offs and identifying Pareto-optimal configurations for deployment. We conclude with a dynamic evaluation based on the random walk drift model to highlight time-correlated risk accumulation.

\section{Empirical Parameter Verification}
\label{sec:empirical_params}

To instantiate the latency models derived in Chapter~\ref{ch:design_methodology}, we first experimentally verify the computational cost ($t_{ver}$) and the theoretical reliability limits ($p_{miss}$) of the encoding mechanisms.

\subsection{Encoding Latency (\texorpdfstring{$t_{ver}$}{t\_ver})}
The encoding time $t_{ver}$ sets the irreducible latency floor for \gls{id}, even when bandwidth is effectively infinite. We measured $t_{ver}$ over 100,000 operations for both \gls{sha} and \gls{rsid}. The results, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:tver_results}, reveal a distinct structural divergence between the two approaches.

\FloatBarrier

\begin{table}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Measured verification time $t_{ver}$ (mean $\pm$ 95\% CI half-width). \gls{sha} shows stable performance for fixed payloads, while \gls{rsid} is sensitive to the verifier length due to field arithmetic complexity.}
    \label{tab:tver_results}
    \begin{tabular}{lrr}
        \toprule
        & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{Verifier Length ($n_{ver}$)}} \\
        \cmidrule(lr){2-3}
        \textbf{System (Payload)} & \textbf{4 bits} & \textbf{16 bits} \\
        \midrule
        \multicolumn{3}{l}{\textit{Cryptographic (SHA-256)}} \\
        \hspace{1em} 96 bits & $2.13 \pm 0.04\,\mu s$ & $2.24 \pm 0.01\,\mu s$ \\
        \hspace{1em} 4001 bits & $14.65 \pm 0.31\,\mu s$ & $15.41 \pm 0.09\,\mu s$ \\
        \midrule
        \multicolumn{3}{l}{\textit{Algebraic (RS-ID)}} \\
        \hspace{1em} 96 bits & $14.55 \pm 0.39\,\mu s$ & $3.79 \pm 0.02\,\mu s$ \\
        \hspace{1em} 4001 bits & $51.78 \pm 0.94\,\mu s$ & $38.57 \pm 0.36\,\mu s$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\FloatBarrier

\gls{sha} demonstrates robust stability; its runtime is dominated by the input block size and is effectively invariant to the output truncation length ($n_{ver}$). In contrast, \gls{rsid} displays a counter-intuitive behavior where \textit{increasing} the verifier length \textit{decreases} latency (e.g., $14.55 \to 3.79\,\mu s$). This is an artifact of the polynomial construction: a larger $n_{ver}$ allows more bits to be packed into each field coefficient, reducing the total number of coefficients ($k$) required to represent the payload.

Table~\ref{tab:pmiss_results} presents the theoretical collision probabilities calculated from the bounds in Chapter~\ref{ch:design_methodology}.

    \label{tab:pmiss_results}
    \begin{tabular}{lrr}

    \end{tabular}

One critical finding is the failure of \gls{rsid} at $n_{ver}=4$. With a field size of only $q=16$, the polynomial degree $d$ for a 96-bit payload exceeds the field size ($d \ge q$), and the bound saturates at $p_{miss}=1.0$. This confirms that algebraic codes require a minimum field headroom to function, establishing a hard lower bound on $n_{ver}$ that does not exist for cryptographic hashes.

The results illustrate the fundamental trade-off between computation and communication. In the transmission-bound regime ($B < 1$ Mbps), latency is dominated by the physical transmission time ($T_{trans}$), and \gls{id} outperforms the baseline by compressing the required transmission from $n_{data}$ to $n_{ver}$. In the computation-bound regime ($B > 10$ Mbps), transmission becomes negligible and the latency floor is set by the encoding time $t_{ver}$; since the traditional scheme has zero encoding overhead, it eventually becomes faster. For large payloads (Figure~\ref{fig:latency_bw_4001}), the break-even point shifts to higher bandwidths, indicating that \gls{id}-based synchronization becomes increasingly valuable as data complexity grows.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\acrshort{lidar}}{LiDAR}-Scale Payload Scalability}
\label{sec:lidar_scale_tver}
The baseline measurements above use small telemetry payloads (96 bits and 4001 bits). For high-dimensional sensors such as \gls{lidar}, the payload size can grow by orders of magnitude. We therefore extend the $t_{ver}$ benchmark to payloads up to 5~MiB (Section~\ref{sec:lidar_scalability_params}).

\input{tables/generated/tver_lidar_scalability_ci_nver16}

Figure~\ref{fig:tver_scaling} (visualized from Table~\ref{tab:tver_lidar_scalability}) highlights a clear crossover in computational efficiency governed by payload size. For \textbf{small payloads} ($< 100$~B), \gls{rsid} exhibits a slight performance edge (e.g., $1.81\,\mu s$ vs. $2.06\,\mu s$ at 96~bits), consistent with a small fixed overhead for polynomial evaluation while \gls{sha} incurs a startup cost due to message padding and the fixed compression routine. For \textbf{large payloads} ($> 1$~KiB), \gls{sha} significantly outperforms \gls{rsid} (e.g., about $2\times$ faster at 5~MiB). While both approaches scale linearly in the number of processed bytes ($O(N)$), \gls{sha} relies primarily on bitwise operations that are heavily optimized on modern CPUs, whereas the current \gls{rsid} implementation is dominated by memory-heavy finite-field multiplication table lookups.

Overall, for high-throughput sensor payloads (\gls{lidar}/video), cryptographic hashing provides superior scalability in this prototype implementation. For distinct control signals and small telemetry payloads, both methods remain computationally negligible (on the order of a few microseconds).

Increasing the tolerance $K$ creates a semantic filter. As seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:kid_mechanisms}(c), moving from $K=2$ to $K=20$ drastically cuts the fallback rate, because the system stops correcting minor sensor noise. This drives the speedup in Fig.~\ref{fig:kid_mechanisms}(b). The trade-off is that a larger acceptance set $\mathcal{A}_K$ increases the chance of accepting an unsafe state through a collision proxy (Fig.~\ref{fig:kid_mechanisms}(a)).

The verifier length $n_{ver}$ is the primary safety knob. It suppresses the conditional miss rate exponentially (linear log-plot in Fig.~\ref{fig:kid_mechanisms}(a)), counteracting the risk introduced by large $K$. In practice, high-tolerance \gls{kid} configurations therefore require longer verifiers to maintain safety.

Figure~\ref{fig:kid_pareto_final} highlights how the assumed noise model changes the attainable safety--performance trade-off. Under the variance-matched Uniform model (Fig.~\ref{fig:kid_pareto_final}b), the distribution is bounded; once the tolerance $K$ exceeds the maximum deviation supported by the model, unsafe events effectively disappear ($P(\mathrm{unsafe})\approx 0$), producing a near-vertical rise close to zero overall risk. This captures the practical case of sensors with known precision limits, where K-ID can be tuned to reduce traffic without materially compromising safety.

Under the Gaussian model (Fig.~\ref{fig:kid_pareto_final}a), the distribution has unbounded support, so there remains a non-zero tail probability of large deviations for any finite $K$. As a result, the frontier forms a smooth convex curve: higher speedups require accepting a gradually increasing (but quantifiable) residual risk. In this setting, selecting $(K,n_{ver})$ becomes a risk-budget decision.

Across both models, $K$ sets the performance scale because it controls how often the protocol falls back, while $n_{ver}$ primarily shifts operating points leftward by reducing collision probability. Table~\ref{tab:kid_pareto_points} lists the Pareto-nondominated configurations for each encoder.

There is no single best parameter set; the optimal choice depends on the application's risk budget. For critical tasks (e.g., human-robot interaction), designers should select points on the far left of Figure~\ref{fig:kid_pareto_final} (e.g., $K=2, n_{ver}=16$), accepting lower speedups ($\approx 1.5\times$) for minimal risk ($< 10^{-11}$). For monitoring tasks (e.g., logging), relaxing constraints toward the right (e.g., $K=10, n_{ver}=12$) yields significant bandwidth savings ($> 4\times$) with manageable risk. Overall, the frontier shows that \gls{kid} enables a controlled and quantifiable trade-off between safety margin and network performance.

\section{Dynamic Evaluation: Random Walk with Drift}
\label{sec:rw_drift_eval}

The parameter sweep in Section~\ref{sec:kid_eval} estimates per-tick risk under an i.i.d. state model. However, safety-critical systems are impacted by \textit{time correlation}: a missed correction can persist and compound as the robot continues moving. We therefore evaluate a dynamic drift model (Section~\ref{sec:random_walk_model}) in which the robot evolves as a random walk and the \gls{dt} uses a ZOH predictor.

\subsection{Qualitative Behavior (Drift \& Escape Time Series)}
Figure~\ref{fig:rw_timeseries} visualizes the \textit{drift and escape} process directly in the state domain. The blue curve shows the robot true state $S_t$ undergoing a random walk, while the orange dashed curve shows the \gls{dt} estimate $\hat{S}_t$ under a ZOH-style reference (held constant). The green band highlights the semantic safety set $\mathcal{A}_K$ and the red background indicates the unsafe region.

The green markers indicate successful detections: when the trajectory breaches the boundary $K$, the protocol triggers fallback and the system is corrected (visualized here as an immediate reset to the reference). The red crosses are the critical events: a hash collision causes a miss exactly at the boundary breach, so the \gls{dt} fails to react and the trajectory continues to drift deeper into the red region. This makes the \textit{accumulating, potentially unbounded} risk visible in a single glance.

\begin{figure*}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/plots/random_walk_drift_gaussian_step2_D15_escape_timeseries.png}
    \caption{Drift-and-escape visualization for the dynamic random walk model ($K=10$). The robot state $S_t$ (blue) performs a random walk. The \gls{dt} estimate $\hat{S}_t$ (orange, dashed) is held constant (ZOH reference). The green band indicates the semantic safety set $\mathcal{A}_K$ and the red background the unsafe region. To quantify time-extended risk, we measure (i) the \textit{breach duration} $L_K$ (consecutive steps with $E_t>K$) and (ii) the \textit{maximum drift} reached before recovery. Figure~\ref{fig:rw_metrics} reports 95th-percentile summaries over multiple runs.

\noindent\textit{Masking vs. collision risk.}
Two distinct mechanisms drive risk as $K$ increases. First, a larger tolerance allows the drift to grow silently for longer (masking), increasing the peak error at the moment verification begins. Second, the miss proxy grows with $K$ (larger acceptable set), meaning that when a boundary breach does occur, it is more likely to be missed for short $n_{ver}$, extending the breach duration. This dynamic view clarifies why low verifier lengths can be dangerous even when the instantaneous collision probability seems acceptable.

\begin{abstract}[pagestyle=empty.tudheadings]%

In time-critical networked control systems, the utility of edge-based digital twins depends on maintaining tight synchronization with physical assets under bandwidth and latency constraints. Traditional periodic full-state transmission is robust but often redundant, because the digital twin typically maintains a local state estimate and does not require the full payload at every tick. This thesis investigates identification-based synchronization and its semantic extension, kinematic identification, which replace payload reconstruction with state verification: the receiver checks whether the remote state lies within a locally predicted tolerance set.

We address these synchronization challenges through a three-stage investigation. First, we establish an end-to-end latency framework that accounts for both computation and transmission delays, and compare truncated cryptographic hashing (the 256-bit variant of the Secure Hash Algorithm 2 family) with Reed--Solomon-based identification coding. For the evaluated software stack, truncated hashing achieves low and largely payload-independent encoding latency, whereas algebraic identification coding incurs higher computation that depends on payload symbolization; the algebraic approach remains relevant for safety-critical deployments that require rigorous mathematical error bounds under stated modeling assumptions.

Second, we operationalize semantic synchronization via a tolerance radius $R$ and map the risk--speedup Pareto frontier. For the studied noise levels, $R\approx 20$ behaves as a performance knee point: further tolerance increases yield diminishing marginal speedup while reducing verifier selectivity. We also identify an entropy-saturation feasibility limit: as the acceptance set grows (e.g., under large tolerance and fine quantization), the finite verifier space at length $n_{ver}$ can become saturated, constraining the achievable miss probability.

Third, we evaluate time-correlated risk under a dynamic random-walk drift model. Using successive recovery intervals to characterize full-state recovery timing, the results reveal a decoupled control logic: tolerance parameters sparsify traffic and modulate the macroscopic synchronization rhythm, while verifier entropy suppresses collision-induced misses and improves the microscopic reliability of state recovery, including the heavy tail of recovery intervals.

Overall, the thesis provides quantitative design guidelines for co-designing semantic tolerance, verifier entropy, and quantization under bandwidth and safety constraints in modern cyber-physical systems.

\keywords{Digital Twins, Kinematic Identification, Semantic Synchronization, Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communications, Networked Control Systems, Entropy Saturation, Successive Recovery Intervals}
    
\end{abstract}

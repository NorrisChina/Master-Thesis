\renewcaptionname{english}{\abstractname}{Abstract}
\begin{abstract}[pagestyle=empty.tudheadings]%

In time-critical \glspl{ncs}, the utility of \glspl{dt} relies strictly on their synchronization accuracy with physical assets. Traditional periodic full-state transmission, while robust, overlooks the predictive capabilities of the \gls{dt}, resulting in significant bandwidth redundancy. To address this inefficiency, this thesis investigates \textbf{\gls{id}-based synchronization}, with a specific focus on the \textbf{\gls{kid}} protocol for semantic compliance. Unlike classical communication aimed at data reconstruction, \gls{kid} transforms the synchronization task into a semantic verification problem: confirming whether the remote state lies within a ``safety tolerance set'' predicted locally.

The main contributions of this thesis are threefold. First, we establish an end-to-end latency model to compare algebraic coding (\gls{rsid}) and cryptographic hashing (\gls{sha}), revealing key engineering trade-offs in edge computing. Experiments demonstrate that truncated \gls{sha} offers stable encoding latency ($\approx 2.2~\mu s$) independent of payload size, making it a superior engineering choice for \gls{urllc} applications compared to \gls{rsid}, which suffers from field saturation at short blocklengths. Second, through extensive parameter sweeps, we map the Risk-Speedup Pareto Frontier of \gls{kid}, proving that introducing moderate semantic tolerance (e.g., $R=10$) can reduce fallback frequency by an order of magnitude, significantly enhancing communication efficiency under bounded risk. Finally, we evaluate error evolution under a dynamic random walk model, uncovering the phenomenon of ``Cascading Failures'' missed by static models: weak verifiers ($n_{ver}=8$) can cause single hash collisions to evolve into persistent boundary breaches. Furthermore, the heavy-tail distribution of Inter-Fallback Times (\gls{ift}) confirms \gls{kid}'s ability to transform continuous sensor streams into sporadic bursty traffic, providing a theoretical foundation for resource scheduling in next-generation semantic communication networks.

\keywords{Digital Twins, K-Identification, Semantic Communication, URLLC, Networked Control Systems, Hash Collision, Random Walk}
    
\end{abstract}
